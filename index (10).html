An audit, not a walk-around

Anyone can look at a car. I check the points where the expensive surprises hide, and I back every finding with a reading, a scan or a photo.

Most buyers already expect some brake or suspension wear on a used car. That is normal, and it's priced in. What buyers can't see coming is unreported accident damage, a battery that's already failing, and leaks or repairs that have been covered up.

After many inspections, I know where those problems hide. Every audit follows the same process, and every red flag I find is photographed and explained in the report.

History

History vs. paint

I pull the Carfax, then measure the paint on every panel with a digital gauge. Each reading is matched to the accident history. Body filler or a repaint with no claim on file points to damage that was never reported.

Scan

Full system scan

A full system scan reads the car's computer modules, not only the engine. Active and permanent faults are flagged. Old, stored codes are listed separately so they don't scare you for nothing.

Battery

Battery and charging

A digital battery tester measures the battery's health, its charge and its cranking power, plus the starter and alternator. A healthy battery that's just flat passes; a worn-out one fails.

MTO

Safety red flags

Grooved rotors, a seeping ball joint, an oil leak, a cracked windshield, bald tires: anything I see that could stop the car from passing a safety is flagged under its MTO category, so you know to get it certified before you buy.

What an inspection can and can't do

I want you to know exactly what you're paying for.

What you get

  • A clear recommendation: recommended, recommended if fixed, or not recommended
  • The Carfax matched against paint readings on every panel, to find unreported damage
  • A full system scan, and a battery and charging system test
  • Red flags that could stop the car from passing a safety, plus tire tread and age
  • The topics to bring up with the dealer, and what Ontario law says they must disclose
  • Photos, the original test printouts, and my opinion as a licensed technician

What it isn't

  • It isn't a warranty. It describes the car on the day I inspect it.
  • I don't take parts apart. Some problems only show up after disassembly.
  • Intermittent problems may not show up during one visit.
  • It isn't a Safety Standards Certificate. Only a licensed inspection station can issue one.

My rule: only buy a used car with a Safety Standards Certificate and an extended warranty. I don't recommend as-is cars. The audit looks for what a safety and a warranty don't cover: unreported damage, a misrepresented history, and problems the seller didn't mention.

    Tips from the bench

    Things I tell every buyer, whether or not they book an inspection.

    AWD

    Service the AWD system

    Transfer case, rear differential and AWD coupling fluids are often skipped because they aren't part of an oil change. Many manufacturers call for them somewhere between 40,000 and 100,000 km, and sooner with towing or hard winters. If the history shows no AWD fluid service on a higher-mileage car, budget for it right away.

    Tires

    Read the DOT date

    The last four digits of the DOT code on the sidewall are the week and year the tire was made. "1717" means week 17 of 2017. Rubber hardens with age, so an old tire can have tread left and still grip poorly in the wet and cold.

    Battery

    Starting isn't passing

    A weak battery can still start a car on a mild day. Only a load test shows how much capacity is left. Ontario winters are when weak batteries fail, so test before you buy.

    Underbody

    Know your undercoating

    Oil-based rust protection is a light brown film that stays wet and seeps into seams. Fresh black spray paint or thick rubberized coating on an older car can hide rust or repairs. Ask what was applied, and when.

    General guidance only. Always check your vehicle's owner's manual for its exact service schedule.

    FAQ

    Straight answers to the questions buyers ask most.

    How soon can you inspect?

    Same day or next day. I inspect after 2:30 PM, and I fit each car in based on my schedule and where it is. There are no time slots to pick: after you book, I confirm the exact time with you and the seller on WhatsApp.

    Do I need to be there?

    No. I travel to wherever the car is in the GTA, whether that's a dealership lot or a private seller's driveway. Your report is sent online, so you can be at work while I inspect.

    How fast do I get the report?

    The same day, as a link you open on your phone plus a PDF. It includes my recommendation, the topics to bring up, the Ontario safety check, the Carfax matched to the paint readings, the system scan and battery test, tire readings and photos.

    What equipment do you use?

    Professional diagnostic equipment: a full system scan tool that reads the car's computer modules, a digital battery and charging system tester, a paint thickness gauge for every panel, and the Carfax Canada history report. My equipment is professional-grade and checked and calibrated daily for accurate readings, and the original printouts are attached to your report.

    What does the report look like?

    A page you open on your phone and scroll through, with a swipeable photo gallery, plus a PDF to keep or forward. See the sample report above for the layout.

    Will you tell me how much to offer?

    No. The report lists the topics to bring up: the findings that matter, like a failed battery, tires under the legal minimum or an active fault. How they're handled is between you and the dealer. A repair done before you buy is usually worth more than a discount.

    Do you inspect cars sold as-is?

    I don't recommend them. An as-is car hasn't passed a safety inspection and may not be fit to drive. My recommendation is always to buy with a Safety Standards Certificate and an extended warranty, and every report says so.

    Can I talk to you about it?

    Yes. If you're busy or want to talk it through, I'll call you on WhatsApp or by phone and give you my honest opinion as a licensed mechanic, including whether I'd buy the car and what to bring up with the dealer before you sign.

    How do you find accident damage the Carfax missed?

    A Carfax only shows damage that was reported. I measure the paint on every panel and graph the readings. On most cars, factory paint sits in a tight range. Factory paint usually reads under 200 µm. 200 to 350 µm means a repaint, and 350 µm and up means body filler. A repainted or filled panel with no claim on the Carfax is a repair that was never reported, and I don't recommend those cars: there's no record of what was damaged, what was replaced or who did the work.

    The car comes with a safety certificate. Do I still need this?

    Yes. Always buy with a safety, but know what it is: a Safety Standards Certificate only confirms the car met Ontario's minimum standard on the day it was certified. It's valid for 36 days and it isn't a warranty. A safety doesn't look at the accident history, the paint, the battery's health or stored faults. My audit flags anything I see that could stop it from passing, and covers what a safety doesn't.

    Does the report cover my rights with the dealer?

    Yes. Ontario dealers must disclose certain facts in writing on the bill of sale, like accident damage over $3,000, past use as a rental or taxi, or a branded title. Your report lists the ones that apply based on what I found, and when you can cancel within 90 days. It isn't legal advice; OMVIC can help with a dispute.

    Will you catch everything?

    No inspection can promise that, and I won't pretend otherwise. I don't take parts apart, and some problems only show up later. The point of the audit is to keep you away from scam cars and unreported damage. For everything else, that's why I recommend a safety certificate and an extended warranty, and why you should budget for maintenance on any used car.

    Do you check tire age?

    Yes. I record tread depth and the DOT date code on every tire, and the report shows each tire's exact age. Ontario's safety standard requires at least 2 mm of tread, and most tire makers set 10 years as the age limit, even with tread left.

    What if the seller won't allow an inspection or a test drive?

    That is a red flag in itself. Reputable dealers allow independent inspections and test drives. I contact the seller to arrange it, so you don't have to.
